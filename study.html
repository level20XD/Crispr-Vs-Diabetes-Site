Diabetic Crispr Treatment

Overview

A study published in the New England Journal of Medicine showed promise in the future application of Crispr technology in diabetic treatment1. The research was led by Sana Biotechnology, a biotechnology company specializing in gene editing1. The study demonstrated how Crispr could be used to edit pancreatic cells to not be rejected by the immune system1.

Procedure

The logo of Sana Biotechnology, the company that led the study on Crispr diabetic treatment.

Source: PR Newswire

The first step of the procedure is to take functional pancreatic cells from a donor. The next step is to edit the DNA of the cells using Crispr technology to make them undetectable by the immune system1. The edited cells are called "hypoimmune," and because they are camaflouged from the immune system, they are much less likely to be attacked1.

Next, the gene-edited cells are transplanted into the patient's body through forearm muscle injection1. Because the cells are hypoimmune, they are less likely to be rejected by the patient's immune system, and so there is less reliance on immunosuppresent drugs1.

Results

The test runs in the study reported that the implanted cells were functional and produced insulin1. Further studies showed that the cells were still stable 12-weeks after the transplantation2. There was no severe immune system response observed2. While this study was successful, there is still more research to be done before this treatment can become availale to the public. For instance, the study only involved one patient who only received a low dose of the treatment1. Still, this study marks a milestone in diabetic treatment and shows promsise in the invention of the cure to type 1 diabetes1.
